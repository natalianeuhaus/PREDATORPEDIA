About the archive

An archive of harm
and accountability.

PREDATORPEDIA brings together documented cases of sexual abuse, allegations against people in positions of power, and the institutional responses that followed. Each entry traces what was reported, how people responded, and what happened next.

The archive draws on public court records, official investigations, and published journalism. Allegations, civil findings, criminal convictions, and institutional decisions are identified separately. The aim is to keep the record visible beyond the headlines.

To suggest a case, provide links to public court records or reporting by an established news organization. Each submission will be reviewed against its sources before publication. A media report or a filed lawsuit does not, by itself, establish that an allegation is true.

Clarence Thomas

U.S. Supreme Court justice
Allegations denied · Senate confirmation was not a criminal verdict
Clarence Thomas, 2007. Steve Petteway, Collection of the Supreme Court of the United States. Public domain. Displayed in black and white with responsive cropping. Photo source ↗

What happened

Clarence Thomas was a federal appeals judge and former chair of the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission when President George H. W. Bush nominated him to the Supreme Court in 1991. In October 1991, law professor Anita Hill testified before the Senate Judiciary Committee that Supreme Court nominee Clarence Thomas had sexually harassed her when she worked for him at the Department of Education and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. Thomas denied her allegations. Hill faced questioning by an all-male, all-white committee in nationally televised hearings.

The response

The hearings brought workplace sexual harassment into national debate and angered women who saw their own experiences dismissed. They also exposed how few women held power in the Senate. Patty Murray later recalled watching the hearings and asking who was speaking for women like her; she decided to run for a Senate seat.

What followed

The Senate confirmed Thomas on October 15, 1991, by 52–48. The confirmation was not a criminal verdict on Hill’s allegations. In the 1992 election, four new women won Senate seats, including Murray, in what became known as the Year of the Woman. Hill’s testimony helped turn public anger into a push for political representation.

1996–2026 / 021996 report → decades of demands

Jeffrey Epstein

Financier
2008 guilty plea · 2019 federal charges ended without trial after his death
Jeffrey Epstein’s Florida mugshot, July 25, 2013. State of Florida / Wikimedia Commons ↗

What happened

Jeffrey Epstein was a wealthy financier whose social circle included prominent figures in business, politics and royalty. In 1996, artist Maria Farmer reported Jeffrey Epstein to the FBI. A complaint dated September 3 recorded allegations involving sexual images of children and threats; its release in 2025 corroborated her early warning. Farmer says she also reported sexual abuse by Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell, but the FBI did not follow up. Palm Beach police began a separate investigation in 2005. In 2007, federal prosecutors agreed not to prosecute Epstein in Florida in exchange for a state guilty plea, without informing victims of the deal. In 2008, he pleaded guilty to two state prostitution-related charges, one involving a minor, and received 18 months in jail. He served about 13 months, with extensive work-release privileges.

The response

Survivors kept pursuing civil cases and speaking publicly. Julie K. Brown’s 2018 Miami Herald investigation brought renewed attention to their accounts and the plea deal. On September 3, 2025, survivors and supporters gathered at the U.S. Capitol to demand the release of investigation records and accountability. The pressure continued decades after the first warning.

What followed

Epstein faced federal sex-trafficking charges in New York in 2019 but died in custody before trial; those charges were dismissed following his death. A 2020 Justice Department review found that prosecutor Alexander Acosta had exercised poor judgment in the earlier deal and that victims had been poorly treated. The Epstein Files Transparency Act became law in November 2025. In January 2026, the Justice Department said its releases totaled nearly 3.5 million pages. Releasing records does not itself establish criminal liability.

1998–2018 / 03January 1998 → a later reckoning

Bill Clinton

U.S. president (1993–2001)
Power imbalance · Impeachment was not a sexual-assault trial
Bill Clinton, 1993. U.S. Government / Congressional Pictorial Directory. Public domain. Displayed in black and white with responsive cropping. Photo source ↗

What happened

Bill Clinton was serving his first term as U.S. president when the relationship began; Lewinsky was starting her career in his workplace. Monica Lewinsky was 22 and a White House intern when her sexual relationship with President Bill Clinton, then 49, began in 1995. It became public in January 1998. Clinton initially denied the relationship and later acknowledged it. In 2018, Lewinsky described the relationship as an abuse of power while explaining that she had wanted the intimacy. Her account placed the enormous disparity between a president and an intern at the center of the story.

The response

Public exposure brought intense scrutiny and humiliation for Lewinsky. The backlash did not automatically become solidarity with her. Years later, she reclaimed her story through writing and anti-bullying advocacy. Her 2015 TED talk challenged the culture of public shaming; the #MeToo movement opened another space to reconsider what had happened to her.

What followed

The House impeached Clinton in December 1998 for perjury and obstruction of justice. The Senate acquitted him on both articles in February 1999, and he remained president. Those proceedings concerned his testimony and conduct during the investigation; they were not a criminal trial for sexual assault. Lewinsky’s later work shifted attention toward power, humiliation, and who bears the lasting consequences.

2010–2026 / 04December 2010 → June 2026

Rex Heuermann

Architect
Guilty pleas and prison sentence
Rex Heuermann. Official booking photograph published with the Suffolk County District Attorney’s June 2026 sentencing announcement. Displayed in black and white with responsive cropping. Photo source ↗

What happened

Rex Heuermann was a Long Island resident and New York City architect who was arrested in 2023 in the Gilgo Beach murder investigation. In December 2010, police searching for missing woman Shannan Gilbert discovered the remains of Melissa Barthelemy, Megan Waterman, Amber Lynn Costello, and Maureen Brainard-Barnes near Gilgo Beach. More remains were discovered in the wider investigation. Several victims were sex workers. Relatives described having their concerns dismissed because of that work, while sex-worker advocates challenged the stigma surrounding the women’s lives and deaths.

The response

Families and friends held a Gilgo Beach vigil in June 2011. That December, sex workers, relatives, and allies gathered at a Manhattan vigil organized around the International Day to End Violence Against Sex Workers. Advocates connected the killings to criminalization, discrimination, and violence against sex workers. The documented response here was vigils and sustained advocacy: families and community organizers kept demanding attention and accountability over many years.

What followed

Rex Heuermann was arrested in July 2023. On April 8, 2026, he pleaded guilty to seven murders and admitted killing an eighth woman, Karen Vergata. On June 17, he received three consecutive sentences of life without parole plus a consecutive 100 years to life. The seven charged victims were Barthelemy, Waterman, Costello, Brainard-Barnes, Jessica Taylor, Sandra Costilla, and Valerie Mack. Shannan Gilbert’s death was not among the murders covered by that plea.

Anthony Weiner

Former U.S. congressman
Guilty plea · Transferring obscene material to a minor
Anthony Weiner, 2011. United States Congress. Public domain. Displayed in black and white with a red tint on scroll and responsive cropping. Photo source ↗

What happened

Anthony Weiner represented a New York district in the U.S. House of Representatives and later sought to become mayor of New York City. Anthony Weiner announced his resignation from Congress in June 2011 after admitting that he had sent explicit photographs and messages to women and had lied about it. Further disclosures disrupted his 2013 campaign for New York mayor. A later, separate case involved a 15-year-old girl: between January and March 2016, he sent her obscene material and directions to engage in sexual conduct despite knowing her age.

The response

Public reporting repeatedly exposed the messages. In September 2016, the girl’s account became public. Federal investigators examined his communications, and the case led to a criminal prosecution. Huma Abedin later told her own story of their marriage, its betrayals, and their family life in her 2021 memoir, Both/And.

What followed

On May 19, 2017, Weiner pleaded guilty to transferring obscene material to a minor. On September 25, he was sentenced to 21 months in federal prison and three years of supervised release. He was also required to register as a sex offender. The conviction concerned his communications with the minor, distinct from the earlier scandals involving adults.

What happened

Bikram Choudhury built an international yoga business around a branded hot-yoga practice and teacher-training courses. This was a civil verdict in her employment case, separate from the rape allegations brought by other women; it was not a criminal rape conviction.

What happened

Andrew, a son of Queen Elizabeth II, served as a British trade envoy and maintained a friendship with Jeffrey Epstein. James Deen

Adult-film performer · Producer
Multiple sexual-assault allegations denied · Professional consequences
James Deen at the 2010 XBIZ Awards. © Glenn Francis, www.PacificProDigital.com. Responsive crop and red tint; this image adaptation is shared under CC BY-SA 3.0. Photo source ↗ CC BY-SA 3.0 ↗

What happened

James Deen was a prominent adult-film performer and producer who had also appeared in mainstream cinema. On November 28, 2015, performer and writer Stoya accused her former partner of rape, saying he ignored her refusal and safeword. Other women came forward with accounts of alleged sexual assault and violations of consent, including Ashley Fires, Tori Lux and Joanna Angel. By early December, reporting counted nine women alleging assaults on and off film sets. Deen denied the allegations, calling them false and defamatory.

The hashtag #SolidarityWithStoya spread as performers and supporters challenged the idea that sex work means blanket consent. The disclosures prompted public discussion of performers’ ability to set limits, stop a scene and report abuse without losing work.

What followed

In January 2016, Kink.com announced revised consent policies, including performers’ right to stop filming and provisions for reporting violations. That same month, Deen received two AVN awards, showing that industry recognition continued despite the allegations.

In March 2016, Cal/OSHA issued nine workplace-safety citations to James Deen Productions and proposed $77,875 in penalties over condom and other safety requirements. These findings concerned worker health and safety; they were not a court determination of the sexual-assault allegations.

The sentence sparked protests and a campaign to recall Persky. Turner was released after serving three months; his sentence also included probation and sex-offender registration. In June 2018, voters removed Persky from office. Miller’s statement and memoir brought the survivor’s account to the center of a case whose coverage had often focused on her attacker.

Donald Trump’s August 24, 2023 booking photograph, Fulton County Sheriff’s Office. This photograph concerns the separate Georgia election-interference case, not the 2016 recording or Carroll’s civil cases. Displayed in black and white with responsive cropping. Photo source ↗

What happened

Donald Trump built his public profile through real estate and television before becoming the Republican presidential nominee in 2016. On October 7, 2016, a recording from a 2005 Access Hollywood appearance became public. Numerous women publicly accused Trump of unwanted kissing, groping and sexual assault. Several women said his public denials prompted them to speak out. Their accounts challenged his description of the recording as merely talk. On January 21, 2017, the day after his inauguration, millions joined Women’s March demonstrations around the world. The marches addressed women’s rights, civil rights and equality; they were not a response to a single allegation.

What followed

Trump won the November 2016 presidential election despite the recording and the public accusations. The 2018 midterms subsequently sent a record number of women to the House of Representatives; 102 held voting seats when the new Congress began in January 2019. That political response did not itself resolve the individual allegations.
